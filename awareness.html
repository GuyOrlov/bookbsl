Access preparation

10 things to check before a Deaf person arrives.

Deaf people are not one group. Some use British Sign Language, some use speech or lipreading, and others may use captions, speech-to-text or a combination.

1

Ask, do not assume

Ask the person how they prefer to communicate and what support is useful.

2

Face the person

Keep your face visible and avoid speaking while looking away.

3

Use good lighting

Make sure faces and hands are clear. Avoid strong backlighting.

4

Speak naturally

Do not shout or exaggerate your mouth movements.

5

One person at a time

This helps group communication, interpreting and captions.

6

Speak to the Deaf person

When an interpreter is present, keep your attention on the Deaf person.

7

Book support early

Specialist communication professionals can be difficult to secure at short notice.

8

Share preparation

Send agendas, slides, scripts, speaker names and specialist vocabulary in advance.

9

Check the environment

Think about sightlines, lighting, screens, captions and room layout together.

10

Check access throughout

Ask whether communication is working instead of assuming the setup is fine.

Room, table & lighting

Set the room up for visual access.

A good layout makes it easier to follow the Deaf person, interpreter, speaker, slides and captions without constant head movement.

Room layout

U-shape, semicircle or open layouts can help groups see each other. Keep the interpreter, main speaker and screen within a comfortable visual area.

Table setup

Keep sightlines clear. Avoid tall flowers, signs, laptops or bottles blocking faces and hands. Leave enough space for the interpreter to work safely.

Lighting

Use even light on faces and hands. Avoid a bright window behind the interpreter. If slides require a darker room, keep the interpreter lit.

Working with interpreters

Make the booking easier to deliver.

Speak to the Deaf person - not to the interpreter.

The interpreter facilitates communication. Speak in the first person and keep your attention on the Deaf person.

Prepare the professional

  • Book as early as possible.
  • Share agendas, slides and terminology.
  • Plan breaks for longer sessions.
  • Tell the provider about recording or livestreaming.
  • Check whether one professional or a team is more suitable.

Common questions

Access preparation FAQ

Does every Deaf person use BSL?

No. Communication preferences vary. Ask the individual what works best for them.

Where should a BSL interpreter sit or stand?

There is no single fixed position. The Deaf person should be able to see the interpreter clearly while also following the speaker, presentation or activity with minimal visual strain.

Should the lights be dimmed during a presentation?

If the room is dimmed, keep enough light on the interpreter, speaker and relevant faces so signing and visual communication remain clear.

What should I send before an interpreted meeting?

Useful preparation may include the agenda, slides, scripts, speaker names, specialist words, venue information and joining instructions.
