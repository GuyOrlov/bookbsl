British Sign Language rights & law · UK guide

What does the law say about British Sign Language access?

A simple overview of the main laws that affect British Sign Language (BSL) users and organisations arranging communication support across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Updated 11 September 2026 · Simple guidance, not legal advice.

The key point: recognising British Sign Language as a language and having a legal duty to provide an interpreter are not exactly the same thing. In many real situations, the practical duty to remove communication barriers comes from disability discrimination law and the duty to make reasonable adjustments.

Two different ideas

Language recognition and reasonable adjustments.

British Sign Language laws recognise and promote the language. Equality and disability law is often what matters when deciding whether an organisation must take practical steps so a Deaf person can access a service, job, event or public function.

LANGUAGE LAW

British Sign Language Acts

These laws recognise and promote British Sign Language and create duties for governments or public bodies to plan, report or take action around the language.

  • They strengthen the status and visibility of British Sign Language.
  • Different Acts apply in different UK nations.
  • They do not automatically mean an interpreter must be supplied for every situation.
ACCESS LAW

Reasonable adjustments

Disability discrimination law can require employers, service providers and public bodies to remove substantial barriers for disabled people.

  • A British Sign Language interpreter can be an appropriate auxiliary service in some circumstances.
  • What is reasonable depends on the situation.
  • For services covered by the Equality Act 2010, a disabled customer cannot be charged for a reasonable adjustment.
Across the UK

Which law applies where?

The legal framework is not identical in every nation. This is a simple overview as at 11 September 2026.

England
Equality Act 2010

Employers, service providers, public bodies and others can have a duty to make reasonable adjustments for disabled people. Depending on the circumstances, communication support such as a British Sign Language interpreter can be part of that duty.

British Sign Language Act 2022

Recognises British Sign Language as a language of Great Britain in its own right and creates reporting and guidance duties around promoting and facilitating its use.

Official British Sign Language Act 2022 ↗
Wales
Equality Act 2010

Applies in Wales, including the reasonable-adjustment framework.

British Sign Language Act 2022

Recognises British Sign Language as a language of Great Britain in its own right.

British Sign Language (Wales) Act 2026

Came into force on 28 April 2026. It creates a Welsh framework to support and encourage the use of British Sign Language, including a National strategy, guidance, a British Sign Language adviser and plans for listed public bodies.

Welsh Government guide ↗
Scotland
Equality Act 2010

Applies in Scotland, including duties to make reasonable adjustments.

British Sign Language (Scotland) Act 2015

Requires Scottish Ministers to prepare British Sign Language national plans and creates planning duties for listed public authorities.

British Sign Language Act 2022

Also recognises British Sign Language as a language of Great Britain in its own right.

Official Scotland Act 2015 ↗
Northern Ireland
Different disability legislation

The Equality Act 2010 does not generally apply in Northern Ireland. Disability protection instead includes the Disability Discrimination Act 1995 and other Northern Ireland legislation, with reasonable-adjustment duties in areas such as employment and services.

Sign Language Act (Northern Ireland) 2026

Received Royal Assent on 9 July 2026. It provides for recognition of British Sign Language and Irish Sign Language in Northern Ireland. Most substantive provisions are brought into operation by commencement order.

For organisers

What should you do when someone asks for a British Sign Language interpreter?

Start with the communication barrier.

If a Deaf British Sign Language user cannot understand, participate or communicate effectively without support, ask what communication support they prefer and consider what adjustment will actually remove the disadvantage.

Important: one solution does not fit every Deaf person. British Sign Language, lipspeaking, speech-to-text, Deaf Relay, deafblind communication support or another adjustment may be appropriate depending on the individual.

1
Ask the Deaf person

Confirm their preferred communication support rather than assuming.

2
Record the adjustment request

Keep the request with the booking information so it is not lost or repeatedly requested.

3
Book early

Qualified British Sign Language interpreters can have limited availability, especially for specialist, evening or large-event work.

4
Give full information

Share the date, times, venue, subject, format, recording plans and preparation materials.

5
Check whether one or two interpreters are appropriate

Duration, intensity, stage work, broadcast work and breaks can affect the recommended team size.
